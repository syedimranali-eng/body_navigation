Every joint is a trade-off between how much it moves and how stable it stays. The nine regions below show that trade-off in action — from the skull's rigid, immovable seams to the shoulder's almost unlimited swing.

FIG. 1 — HEAD & JAW

Head & Jaw

The skull itself is mostly joint-free by design: its plates are locked together at sutures, fibrous seams that fuse over childhood so the brain has a rigid case. The one skull joint that moves freely is the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), just in front of each ear, where the lower jaw hangs from the skull.

The TMJ combines a hinge motion (opening and closing) with a small forward glide, which is why the jaw can drop straight down and also slide slightly forward to widen the bite — useful for chewing and speech.

SUTURE — FIXEDTMJ — HINGE+GLIDE
FIG. 2 — NECK

Neck

The neck is where the spine gets its most specialized joints. The very top joint, between the skull and the first vertebra (the atlas), lets you nod "yes." Just below it, the joint between the first and second vertebrae (the atlas and axis) is a pivot joint — a bony ring rotating around a peg — and it's responsible for almost all of the "no" head-turn.

Lower down, the remaining cervical vertebrae stack on fibrocartilage discs and glide against each other at small facet joints, adding the rest of the neck's tilt and rotation in smaller increments.

FIG. 3 — SHOULDER

Shoulder

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and the diagram shows why: the socket on the shoulder blade is shallow, more like a saucer than a cup, so the ball at the top of the arm bone is barely held in place by bone alone.

That shallow fit is what lets you swing your arm through nearly a full circle — overhead, behind you, across your chest. The trade-off is stability: the shoulder relies heavily on surrounding muscles and ligaments to stay in place, and it's the joint most prone to dislocation.

BALL-AND-SOCKETSHALLOW SOCKET
FIG. 4 — ELBOW

Elbow

The elbow is a straightforward hinge: the end of the upper-arm bone fits into a notch on the forearm bone, and the two move in a single plane, like a door on a pin. The dashed "ghost" position in the diagram shows how far that swing carries the forearm.

A second joint at the same location — between the two forearm bones — lets the forearm twist independently, which is what turns your palm up or down without involving the elbow's hinge at all.

HUMEROULNAR — HINGERADIOULNAR — PIVOT
FIG. 5 — WRIST & HAND

Wrist & Hand

The wrist joint itself is a condyloid joint — an oval bone-end rocking inside a shallow oval socket — which allows up-down and side-to-side motion but not rotation. Between the small wrist bones themselves are several tiny plane joints that glide slightly, giving the wrist its overall flexibility.

Out in the hand, the knuckles (where fingers meet the palm) are condyloid joints too, while the finger joints beyond them are simple hinges. The one exception is the base of the thumb, a saddle joint, which is what lets the thumb swing across the palm to touch the fingertips.

WRIST — CONDYLOIDKNUCKLES — CONDYLOIDFINGERS — HINGETHUMB BASE — SADDLE
FIG. 6 — BACK / SPINE

Back & Spine

No single joint gives the back its flexibility — the spine bends by adding up dozens of small movements. Between each pair of vertebrae sits a fibrocartilage disc, a symphysis joint that compresses slightly and lets the vertebrae tilt a few degrees relative to each other.

Behind those discs, small facet joints (plane joints) at the back of each vertebra glide against their neighbors, guiding the direction of movement and limiting rotation at any single level. Stack enough of these small movements together and you get the spine's full range: bending forward, arching back, and twisting side to side.

DISCS — SYMPHYSISFACET JOINTS — PLANE
FIG. 7 — HIP

Hip

The hip is built on the same ball-and-socket plan as the shoulder, but the socket here wraps much further around the ball — closer to a true cup than a saucer. Compare the depth of the curve in this diagram with the shoulder's shallower one in Fig. 3.

That extra bony coverage costs the hip some range of motion compared to the shoulder, but it makes the joint far more inherently stable. It's a large part of why hip dislocations are rare and usually require serious trauma, while shoulder dislocations are common.

BALL-AND-SOCKETDEEP, STABLE SOCKET
FIG. 8 — KNEE

Knee

The knee is the largest joint in the body, and mostly behaves like a hinge — bending and straightening in one main plane, shown by the long swing in the diagram. Two crescent-shaped cartilage pads, the menisci, sit between the thigh and shin bones to cushion that motion (the small shaded wedge at the joint line).

It isn't a pure hinge, though: once the knee is bent, the shin bone can rotate slightly inward and outward (the small curved arrow at the joint line), a detail that's stabilized by the knee's ligaments, including the cruciate ligaments crossing inside the joint itself.

MODIFIED HINGEMENISCI — CARTILAGE
FIG. 9 — ANKLE & FOOT

Ankle & Foot

The ankle's main joint, between the shin bones and the top of the foot, is a hinge — it's what lets you point your toes and pull your foot back up toward your shin, shown by the dashed alternate position in the diagram.

Just below that, a separate joint (the subtalar joint) and a row of small joints between the foot bones handle a different motion entirely: rolling the sole of the foot inward and outward. Splitting the work between these joints is what lets the foot both flex like a hinge and adapt to uneven ground.

The Hand & Finger Joint System

The hand packs more joints into a small space than almost anywhere else in the body, and each one is tuned for a different job. The eight joints below move from the wrist's load-bearing sway down to the fingertip's simple one-way hinge, showing how that division of labor adds up to a hand that can both grip hard and thread a needle.

FIG. 1 — WRIST (RADIOCARPAL JOINT)

Wrist (Radiocarpal Joint)

The main wrist joint sits between the rounded end of the radius and the oval face of the first row of carpal bones — an oval fitting into a shallow oval socket, which makes it a condyloid joint. That shape allows the hand to rock forward and back and tilt side to side, but not to twist independently; any rotation you feel at the wrist actually comes from the radius and ulna twisting around each other further up the forearm.

Because every load the hand carries has to pass through this one joint on its way to the arm, it's built more for controlled stability than for the wide swing seen further out in the fingers — a trade-off that becomes clearer at each joint further down this report.

RADIOCARPAL — CONDYLOIDFLEXION/EXTENSIONRADIAL/ULNAR DEVIATION
FIG. 2 — INTERCARPAL & MIDCARPAL JOINTS

Intercarpal & Midcarpal Joints

Between the wrist joint and the palm sit eight small carpal bones, arranged in two rows of four. Every bone touches its neighbors through a flat plane joint, and the seam between the two rows — the midcarpal joint — lets the whole distal row glide slightly against the whole proximal row.

No single one of these joints moves very far on its own, much like the small facet joints between vertebrae in the spine. But eight small glides stacked together give the wrist its rounded, cushioned feel of motion and let it absorb shock that would otherwise transmit straight through to the forearm.

INTERCARPAL — PLANEMIDCARPAL — GLIDE
FIG. 3 — THUMB CARPOMETACARPAL JOINT

Thumb Carpometacarpal Joint

At the base of the thumb, the metacarpal meets the trapezium bone through a saddle joint — two surfaces that are each concave in one direction and convex in the other, interlocking like a rider sitting in a saddle. It's the rarest joint shape in the body, and the hand only has one of them.

That saddle shape is what lets the thumb swing across the palm to touch each fingertip — a movement called opposition that no other digit's base joint can perform. It's also what makes precision grip possible at all, though the same mobility leaves this joint especially prone to wear, which is why thumb-base arthritis is so common later in life.

CARPOMETACARPAL (THUMB) — SADDLEOPPOSITION
FIG. 4 — FINGER CARPOMETACARPAL JOINTS

Finger Carpometacarpal Joints

Where the four finger metacarpals meet the carpal row, the joints are flat plane joints rather than saddles — and they're not all equally mobile. The index and middle finger joints are bound tightly by ligaments and barely move, giving the hand a rigid central platform to push and pinch against.

The ring and little finger joints, by contrast, have noticeably more give, shown by the wider arcs in the diagram. That extra glide is what lets the ulnar side of the palm fold inward, cupping around a ball or a handle in a way the index and middle side of the hand simply can't.

CMC (FINGERS) — PLANEVARIABLE MOBILITY
FIG. 5 — METACARPOPHALANGEAL JOINTS

Metacarpophalangeal Joints (Knuckles)

The big knuckles — where each finger meets the palm — are condyloid joints: the rounded head of the metacarpal rocks inside a shallow socket at the base of the finger's first bone. Held straight, as in the diagram's smaller side arc, the fingers can also fan apart and together at this joint.

That side-to-side spread disappears once the fingers curl into a fist, because the ligaments on either side of the joint tighten as it bends and lock the knuckle into a single hinge-like plane. It's a small mechanical detail that makes a clenched fist far more stable than an open hand.

METACARPOPHALANGEAL — CONDYLOIDFLEXION + ABDUCTION
FIG. 6 — PROXIMAL INTERPHALANGEAL JOINTS

Proximal Interphalangeal Joints

Moving out along the finger, the joint between the first and second bones is a pure hinge — no side-to-side motion at all, just a wide swing in a single plane, shown by the long arc in the diagram. It's the largest range of motion of any joint in the finger, often bending past 100 degrees.

Because it does most of the work of curling the finger into a hook or a fist, this joint takes a lot of mechanical strain, and it's stabilized by tight collateral ligaments and a fibrous plate on its palm side that stops it from bending backward. It's also the joint most often "jammed" in ball sports.

PROXIMAL INTERPHALANGEAL — HINGE
FIG. 7 — DISTAL INTERPHALANGEAL JOINTS

Distal Interphalangeal Joints

The joint closest to the fingertip is also a hinge, but a noticeably shallower one — the shorter arc in the diagram reflects a smaller range of motion than its neighbor further up the finger. It's driven by a single tendon that runs the full length of the forearm to reach it.

Fine as its movement is, this joint does a disproportionate share of the hand's precision work: the last small adjustment of pressure and angle when pinching a coin, threading a needle, or typing. Its narrow range also makes it more vulnerable to jamming from a straight-on impact, such as a fingertip catching a ball.

DISTAL INTERPHALANGEAL — HINGE
FIG. 8 — THUMB INTERPHALANGEAL JOINT

Thumb Interphalangeal Joint

The thumb breaks the pattern seen in the other four fingers: it has only two bones instead of three, so it has just one interphalangeal joint instead of two. Like its counterparts in the fingers, it's a simple hinge, bending and straightening in a single plane.

On its own it's an unremarkable joint, but it's the last link in a chain — saddle joint at the base, condyloid joint at the knuckle, hinge here at the tip — that together let the thumb pivot, spread, and curl into the precise pinch grip the rest of the hand is built around.
